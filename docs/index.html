01 — Mechanism

One loop, two chains, no off-chain bot in the fast path.

A reactive contract on Reactive Network's Lasna watches the same WETH/HTRG pool on two L2s and fires the trade on the cheaper venue when the price gap clears the round-trip cost.

Pools · Base & Unichain Sepolia

Two pools, one token

An identical WETH/HTRG constant-product pool on Base Sepolia (84532) and Unichain Sepolia (1301): same seed, same starting price, canonical Uniswap V3 Swap events.

Decision · Lasna

Where the gap is decided

The reactive contract subscribes to both pools, stores the last price per venue, and computes the cross-chain gap in basis points. It requests a callback only when the gap clears the round-trip cost (150 bps threshold, 30 s cooldown).

Fill · cheaper venue

Where the trade lands

An executor on the destination re-validates and fills. The fast arm has no off-chain bot: the reactive callback is the trigger, and the measured latency is reported per round, not assumed.

02 — The experiment

Every gap produces two real fills. The second is held back seven seconds.

The control arm executes the identical trade, unconditionally — it does not share the fast arm's slippage guard, so its outcome is a measurement rather than a deterministic function of tolerance.

Fast arm callback

The reactive contract observes a Swap on the origin chain, computes the cross-chain gap, and requests a callback. The executor on the cheaper venue fills as soon as the callback lands.

end-to-end median · 2 s

Control arm +7 s

A keeper waits until the destination block timestamp is at least seven seconds past detection, then runs the same trade. It fills whatever the pool offers and is scored on the realized fill — no slippage guard.

end-to-end median · 12 s

One bias, stated up front: because we are the only participant, the fast arm's own fill is part of the pool state the control faces. The control is measured against a slower copy of ourselves, which is conservative for the control. Read the column as “what a 7 s-late version of this system gets after the 2 s version has already traded.”

The control losing all 23 is the point: x* consumes the gap down to the two-fee band by construction, so the +7 s copy replays the same size into a collapsed gap and pays the fee twice. In Version 1 the fixed 0.0004 WETH under-sized wide gaps, left profit on the table, and the late copy still cleared 27 of 28. Sizing to the gap is what removes its edge. Gaps here are wide (226–805 bps) — a synthetic-flow artifact; the finding is about sizing, not magnitude.

04 — Break-even

Where a slower fill stops being worth taking.

Net profit tracks the detected gap almost one-for-one. Plotting realized net against the gap at detection locates the point where each arm stops covering the round-trip — the 30 bps fee plus the price impact of the order itself.

Fast arm (2 s) Control arm (+7 s) Fitted region — not measured
Crossover, measured on consecutive rounds as the gap converged. Round 27 (460 bps): both profit. Round 32 (162 bps): the delayed fill nets −30 bps while the fast fill still nets +72 bps. The fast break-even is extrapolated to ≈91 bps and the control to ≈206 bps. Below 162 bps the fast floor is fitted, not measured — that is the smallest gap this run produced.
05 — Timing, stated honestly

What the two seconds are made of.

Timestamps are whole-second EVM block times; cross-chain clocks are not synchronized. We report what the chains report, and we make no claim finer than one second.

−1…0 s

Observation leg

Origin swap → Lasna reaction reads −1 to 0 s in all 28 rounds. That cannot be true elapsed time; it is quantization between two unsynchronized clocks, reported as a bound, not a signed latency.

2–3 s

Callback leg — the bottleneck

Lasna reaction → destination fill. This is Reactive callback delivery plus block inclusion. Because it is counted from a start point about one second after the origin swap, this sub-leg can read one second above the full end-to-end span.

±1 s

No claim finer than a second

A one-second fill and a 1.9-second fill are indistinguishable on-chain. The 2 s median is 19 of 28 rounds at exactly 2 s; the defensible statement is the measured span, quantized.

The delivery floor is not a fee we can bid away. In the callback configuration gasLimit is a compute ceiling, not a priority bid. A no-op reactive that does nothing but request a trivial callback arrived in the same 2–3 s as the trading callback (median callback leg 3 s over 20 deliveries; the arb's own callback leg is 3 s over 28 rounds) — and running it at 200,000 gas against the arb's 400,000 changed nothing. Reactive's documentation states that a higher gasLimit does not accelerate delivery or increase priority, and the config carries only chainId, recipient, gasLimit and payload, with no priority field (dev.reactive.network). So the 2–3 s callback leg on the Lasna→Unichain path in our runs is relayer transit plus destination block inclusion — the floor of that path, not a number we tuned.

Profit is mark-to-market: HTRG bought on the destination is valued at the origin venue's post-swap spot price. Nothing is sold and nothing crosses a bridge; the tokens are independent deployments. The experiment measures whether the decision arrived before the edge decayed.
